# Viral Product Principles

Source supplied by the user. The author frames these as patterns learned from building startups in public: use them as a compass, not a checklist.

## Acquisition and first impression

1. **No free plan by default.** Free users can add support and infrastructure cost while rarely converting. Validate whether a free tier serves acquisition or only creates drag.
2. **Use three colors.** Keep the palette focused: neutral text, neutral background, and one action color.
3. **Use numbers instead of adjectives.** Replace vague claims such as “fast” with measurable outcomes such as “save 4 hours every week.”
4. **End with a shareable footer.** Most visitors will not buy, but a memorable ending can earn sharing.
5. **Treat the OG image like a YouTube thumbnail.** The social preview may receive more impressions than the page; design it to earn the click.
6. **Reveal one idea per screen.** Each viewport should communicate one main message.
7. **Write a headline a fifth grader can understand.** Simple words protect curiosity and comprehension.
9. **Use copy only this product could write.** Ground language in specific experience, customers, constraints, and mechanisms.
10. **Show the product before explaining it.** A representative demo can outperform paragraphs of description.
13. **Ride an existing wave.** Connect to technologies and problems people already discuss when the fit is authentic.
14. **Borrow the best copy from customers.** Use customers' language and jobs-to-be-done rather than internal jargon.
15. **Make the founder visible and audible.** Human explanation can outperform generic corporate promotion when founder trust matters.
17. **Write a headline people remember tomorrow.** Test multiple headlines for delayed recall, not immediate preference only.
18. **Use an emotional headline.** Create a human reaction instead of listing features.
19. **Do something people have not seen before.** A clone rarely earns word of mouth; create authentic novelty.
20. **Make the hero capable of selling the product.** The hero must explain the product and create desire within seconds.
21. **Show empathy before selling.** Describe the user's problem accurately enough to earn trust before presenting the solution.
23. **Use a memorable name.** Prefer familiar words and names that do not require explanation.
24. **Sell a human desire, not a feature.** Connect features to time, money, health, status, safety, or reduced pain.
26. **Avoid weak words.** Replace “most,” “many,” and “rarely” with claims people can picture, remember, and challenge.
29. **Do not launch without proof.** Collect genuine feedback or testimonials before sending traffic; never fabricate them.
30. **Describe the product in under ten words.** If users cannot repeat the proposition, they cannot spread it.
31. **Compare against alternatives.** Show why switching is worthwhile using criteria customers actually value.
32. **Consider premium pricing.** Higher price can signal differentiation, but only when value, proof, market, and procurement support it.

## Focus and action

11. **Do one thing.** Be known for the specific problem the product solves, not for a long feature inventory.
22. **Use one primary call to action.** Multiple competing paths create hesitation; privilege one next step.
28. **Make the call to action describe what happens next.** Replace generic labels such as “Get Started” with specific outcomes such as “Analyze My Website.”

## Pricing and monetization

8. **Consider a hard paywall.** Payment can test willingness to pay before collecting extensive user data. Balance this against trust and time-to-value.
12. **Use Popcorn Pricing.** Keep the decision legible with Good, Better, Best rather than a dense spreadsheet of tiers.
16. **Make pricing easy to find.** Prospects use price to understand the product as well as the cost.
25. **Let people try the product before buying.** Put representative value on the landing page so people can experience the product before paying.
27. **Question subscriptions.** Prefer a one-time payment when ongoing delivery and cost do not justify recurring billing.

## Conflict map

- Principles 1 and 8 can conflict with principle 25. Resolve with trust requirements, cost-to-serve, time-to-value, and conversion evidence.
- Principle 27 can conflict with a product that has recurring infrastructure, support, compliance, or continuously delivered value.
- Principle 2 is a hierarchy heuristic, not a ban on semantic status colors, charts, warnings, dark mode, or accessibility affordances.
- Principle 22 means one dominant next action per decision context, not removal of necessary navigation or recovery paths.
- Principles 3, 26, 29, 31, and 32 require truthful evidence. Never manufacture specificity, testimonials, comparisons, or premium positioning.
